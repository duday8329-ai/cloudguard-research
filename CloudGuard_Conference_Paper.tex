\begin{abstract}
Infrastructure as Code (IaC) makes cloud deployment repeatable, but it can also reproduce insecure settings. This paper presents CloudGuard, a browser-based analyzer for Terraform and Amazon Web Services (AWS) CloudFormation that helps reviewers trace configuration-policy violations before deployment. The prototype applies five deterministic text-pattern rules and presents policy identifiers, source evidence, severity, heuristic risk deductions, control references, remediation guidance, and a local policy gate. It supports repair previews for selected source patterns but does not deploy to AWS or claim general automatic repair. We evaluated CloudGuard on a project-team synthetic benchmark of 50 manifests (25 Terraform and 25 CloudFormation) containing 70 labelled policy instances. Cases cover a potential Amazon S3 public-ACL setting, an enabled Amazon Relational Database Service (RDS) public-access flag, unrestricted security-group ingress, wildcard identity and access management actions, and disabled database encryption. CloudGuard recorded 30 true positives, no false positives, no false negatives, and 40 true negatives, yielding precision, recall, and F1 of 1.0000. Ten command-line batch scans over the benchmark had a median of 180.761 ms and a nearest-rank 95th-percentile time of 234.185 ms. Separately, 30 positive synthetic cases passed structural and target-rule-absence checks after controlled source replacement. These findings are implementation sanity evidence, not production accuracy, generalization, calibrated risk prediction, semantic repair correctness, or deployment safety. The offline checks do not measure browser repair quality; energy use, sustainability, and developer usability were not measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Cloud infrastructure is increasingly declared in machine-readable configuration rather than provisioned manually. This improves repeatability, but it also allows a permissive access rule or disabled protection to be copied across environments before deployment review. Security scanners can identify many conditions, yet their output is often difficult for a developer or reviewer to connect to a concrete policy and next action.

CloudGuard addresses this traceability problem with a small, inspectable AWS IaC analyzer. Security scanners such as Checkov, KICS, and Trivy are commonly used for IaC security analysis~\cite{checkov,kics,trivy}. The prototype reads Terraform and CloudFormation files locally in the browser, applies five explicit rules, computes a transparent score from configured deductions, and displays remediation guidance. It is a capstone research artifact and demonstration, not a production cloud security service.

Minna et al.~\cite{minna2026automated} investigated inconsistent security-policy analysis and policy mapping across analyzers for Helm charts in Kubernetes environments. CloudGuard studies the related traceability problem for AWS Terraform and CloudFormation, without implementing Helm or Kubernetes support.

The contributions are: (1) a traceable policy-instance representation for local AWS IaC review; (2) an explainable finding workflow that preserves policy ID, severity, source evidence, heuristic risk deductions, control reference, and remediation guidance; (3) a reproducible synthetic mutation benchmark and cross-tool normalization procedure for implementation sanity testing; and (4) controlled repair previews and local policy gating presented as supporting engineering controls rather than general repair claims. The objectives are limited to detecting five selected conditions, preserving this evidence chain, regenerating the retained benchmark metrics, and supporting cautious local review.

\section{Related Work}
CloudGuard is positioned among infrastructure-analysis tools and cloud security guidance rather than as a replacement for them. Prior work has surveyed IaC static analysis~\cite{chiari2022survey}, catalogued security smells~\cite{rahman2019sins}, studied risky infrastructure scripts~\cite{dai2020risky}, and examined architecture-level security practices~\cite{ntentos2022conformance}. These studies establish the importance of rule-based and structural analysis. Checkov, KICS, and Trivy provide established scanning approaches~\cite{checkov,kics,trivy}, while Minna et al.~\cite{minna2026automated} examine policy analysis for Helm charts. Related cloud-forensics research also highlights the value of preserving evidence during cloud investigations~\cite{sukumar2026forensics}; CloudGuard applies a narrower, pre-deployment evidence workflow to Terraform and CloudFormation source files. CloudGuard does not claim that existing tools lack evidence, severity, or remediation features; its contribution is to combine a particular set of traceable records and local review steps, then test that workflow on a small, auditable policy-instance benchmark.

\section{Proposed Method}
\subsection{Scope and Evidence Boundary}
CloudGuard supports Terraform and CloudFormation~\cite{terraform,cloudformation} only. It does not support Helm or Kubernetes, does not execute changes against AWS, and does not claim general automatic program repair. The selected file is read in the browser, matched against deterministic rules, and rendered as a report, repair preview, and local policy decision.
The evaluation is intentionally framed as an implementation study. It checks whether the selected predicates, evidence records, metric pipeline, baseline normalization, and controlled review workflow operate consistently on the retained benchmark. It does not claim user-study evidence, production generalization, calibrated risk prediction, or validated automatic repair.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing Analyzers}
CloudGuard does not treat a similar rule name as proof of equivalence with another scanner. A comparison is made only when a native rule's documented condition matches a CloudGuard predicate for the same resource and format. This semantic mapping is retained as an auditable record and is later used to calculate coverage and mapped metrics separately.

\subsection{System Design}
The user selects a \texttt{.tf}, \texttt{.yaml}, \texttt{.yml}, or \texttt{.json} file, or loads the built-in vulnerable sample. The browser places the text in the editor. Matching rules become finding records. The renderer displays a report, a 0--100 score, remediation recommendations, a repair preview when a safe transformation is available, and a configurable local policy decision. No cloud credentials or deployment operation is required.

The implementation defines five policy conditions: POL\_AWS\_S3\_PUBLIC, POL\_AWS\_RDS\_PUBLIC, POL\_AWS\_SG\_UNRESTRICTED, POL\_AWS\_IAM\_WILDCARD, and POL\_AWS\_RDS\_ENCRYPTION. The selected controls are aligned with AWS security guidance~\cite{awswell,s3public,rdsnetwork,sg-rules,iam-action}. The score begins at 100 and subtracts 28, 25, 18, 18, and 14 points for these findings, respectively. These deductions are CloudGuard-defined heuristic weights selected to provide transparent relative prioritization; they are not calibrated probabilities of compromise.

\subsection{Policy Semantics and Risk Computation}
The matcher applies deterministic text patterns intended to identify selected resource-property conditions. It scans source text for Terraform assignments such as \texttt{acl = "public-read"}, \texttt{publicly\_accessible = true}, \texttt{storage\_encrypted = false}, \texttt{cidr\_blocks = ["0.0.0.0/0"]}, and wildcard IAM actions, together with corresponding CloudFormation properties. The S3 ACL value is treated as a potential public-access misconfiguration indicator, not proof of effective external accessibility. Similarly, \texttt{publicly\_accessible = true} is an Amazon Relational Database Service (RDS) public-access flag, not proof that the database is reachable from the public Internet; effective reachability also depends on network configuration. The CIDR expression is not context-parsed as ingress; an identical literal in egress, comments, or unrelated text could be matched. Comments are not stripped, and variables, modules, intrinsic functions, indirection, and unsupported syntax are not resolved. Missing or ambiguous properties are not silently converted into violations.

For a file containing findings $v_1,\ldots,v_n$, the displayed score is
\begin{equation}
S=\max\left(0,100-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(v_i)\right),
\end{equation}
where $w(v_i)$ is the configured heuristic deduction for the policy family. In the current browser implementation, the rule matcher emits at most one finding per policy SID for a file, so repeated text matches within the same policy family do not create repeated deductions. The maximum operation prevents a negative display score. This design makes the reasoning inspectable: a reviewer can identify the finding, read its source evidence, and reproduce the deduction. The score is intentionally not interpreted as a probability, expected loss, or compliance percentage.

\subsection{Design Principles}
Each finding retains a stable SID, source evidence, severity, heuristic deduction, and remediation guidance. Detection is separated from change approval, and every operation is local and reversible: a reviewer can inspect the input, preview a limited transformation, re-scan a copy, download it, or reject it without modifying cloud resources.

Architecturally, the browser prototype separates five logical responsibilities: local source input, deterministic SID-level policy detection, evidence and heuristic risk prioritization, repair guidance with re-scanning, and the final local \texttt{ALLOW}/\texttt{BLOCK} policy decision. These are logical responsibilities within one prototype, not independently deployed services.

\subsection{Repair and Local Policy Controls}
The implementation separates detection from change approval. For a limited set of source patterns, CloudGuard creates a controlled deterministic repair preview, re-scans the preview, and permits the user to download the repaired copy. Ambiguous access-control findings are not given invented replacements: the interface supplies copyable manual guidance for selecting an approved CIDR, security-group reference, or least-privilege IAM action set.

The local policy gate evaluates the findings from the selected source file or its re-scanned modified copy against a user-selected severity threshold. A finding at or above the threshold produces \texttt{BLOCK}; otherwise the result is \texttt{ALLOW}. The repository exposes the same decision through a local gate script that could be wrapped by CI, but CI integration is not validated. The gate blocks a proposed configuration review; it does not deploy to AWS and does not replace native IaC validation.

A developer selects a source file, inspects findings and evidence, and uses the score and severity to prioritize review. A supported source pattern can produce a separate repair preview for re-scanning; ambiguous access-control changes stop at copyable guidance. The workflow is local and auditable, but it does not replace code review or deployment testing.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The research package contains a 50-manifest project-team synthetic mutation benchmark: 25 Terraform and 25 CloudFormation manifests, with 70 labelled policy-instance pairs. A policy instance associates a file, resource, format, policy SID, expected violation label, and severity. This prevents the evaluation from treating one file as a single result when several policies occur in the same file. In the frozen benchmark, each file contains one relevant resource for each represented policy family, and an audit of the retained consensus file found 70 rows with no repeated file--policy-SID pair. The current implementation emits at most one prediction per file and policy SID, and the scoring join uses that same file--SID key; therefore, the implementation and evaluation units are consistent for this benchmark. Multi-resource files containing repeated instances of the same SID remain an identified extension for future evaluation.

The labels were produced by the project team using predefined criteria. Two reviewer files are retained as an audit trail, but the collection process is not documented as independent blinded annotation. Therefore, this paper does not report Cohen's kappa or claim inter-rater reliability. The benchmark is suitable for regression testing and implementation sanity checks, but not for generalization to arbitrary production configurations.

Results are regenerated from retained labels and predictions. The public repository is \url{https://github.com/duday8329-ai/cloudguard-research}.

\subsection{Threat Model and Non-Goals}
The threat model is configuration-level misconfiguration before cloud deployment. The prototype assumes a local Terraform or CloudFormation file, not AWS accounts, runtime logs, identity data, network telemetry, or state files; it therefore cannot detect runtime compromise, drift, or interactions requiring a provider semantic model. CloudGuard is not a replacement for Checkov, KICS, Trivy, native validation, change-set review, or organizational approval, and it does not claim complete AWS coverage or safe automatic repair.

\subsection{Metric Definitions}
For CloudGuard, each policy-instance prediction is compared with the expected violation label. Precision, recall, and F1 are calculated as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
F1=2\frac{\mathrm{Precision}\times\mathrm{Recall}}
{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\end{equation}
Baseline analyzer metrics are calculated only after validated semantic mappings are established. Unmapped policy-format pairs are excluded from the mapped denominator and reported as coverage limitations, not as true negatives. Latency is measured over repeated local analyzer runs and reported with the environment and trial count.

\subsection{Scanner Normalization Protocol}
The retained baseline runs used Checkov 3.3.22, KICS 2.1.20, and Trivy 0.74.0 on the same frozen benchmark files. Native outputs were retained before normalization, and the mapping file records the tool rule identifier, format, CloudGuard SID, and mapping status. The unmapped pairs are listed in that file: Checkov and KICS have no validated CloudFormation IAM-wildcard mapping, while Trivy has no validated IAM-wildcard mapping for either format. The reported comparison is therefore a versioned, coverage-led synthetic comparison rather than an assertion that the tools are interchangeable.
The baseline comparison uses a two-stage process. First, the native output of each scanner is retained without editing. Second, a normalization record maps a native rule identifier to a CloudGuard SID only when the rule's documented condition matches the CloudGuard predicate for the same resource and format. Name similarity alone is not sufficient evidence for a mapping. For example, a scanner rule about public S3 access can map to \texttt{POL\_AWS\_S3\_PUBLIC} only when its trigger condition corresponds to the public-access condition used by the benchmark. A rule that reports a broader or different condition remains unmapped.

CloudGuard's actual scoring script joins predictions and labels by $\langle\text{file\_id},\text{policy\_sid}\rangle$ because the prototype prediction record does not include a resource ID. The benchmark has no repeated file--policy-SID pair, so this key is unambiguous for the current run. Baseline normalization retains $\text{resource\_id}$ when a tool provides it and uses the file--SID fallback when it does not; the mapping artifact records this limitation. The join produces TP, FP, FN, or TN. Coverage is computed separately as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}=\frac{\text{mapped policy instances}}{\text{all labelled policy instances}}.
\end{equation}
This separation prevents an absent scanner rule from being mistaken for a correct negative prediction. It also explains why a tool can have perfect mapped F1 while still covering fewer policy instances than CloudGuard.

The repository stores the benchmark files, policy-instance labels, reviewer files, SID mapping, raw scanner outputs, normalized predictions, confusion matrices, latency trials, remediation records, and generated summaries in separate directories. The reproduction script reads these artifacts rather than accepting manually typed values for the reported tables. Each result should therefore be traceable from a table cell to a raw prediction and then to a benchmark row. The package records the local Python version, operating system, trial count, and benchmark status so that a future run can distinguish a reproduced sanity result from a new experiment.

\section{Results}
The CloudGuard sanity run generated 30 true positives, no false positives, no false negatives, and 40 true negatives over 70 policy-instance labels. Precision, recall, and F1 are all 1.0000. Because the mutation cases were designed to exercise the same five prototype rules, this result is expected to be optimistic. It demonstrates that the pipeline, labels, predictions, and metric generation work together; it does not demonstrate perfect performance on unseen AWS IaC. The zero false positives apply only to the retained benchmark and do not establish a false-positive rate for comments, unrelated text, or context-sensitive configurations.

\subsection{Latency and Component Sensitivity}
Ten local CloudGuard trials gave a median processing time of 180.761 ms and a nearest-rank p95 time of 234.185 ms on Windows 11 with Python 3.13.15. Each trial timed \texttt{run\_cloudguard.py}, including scanning all 50 files and writing the prediction CSV; browser rendering was not timed. The script uses nearest-rank p95, $x_{\lceil0.95n\rceil}$ after sorting, so the ten-trial estimate selects the maximum and remains exploratory. Individual measurements are retained in \texttt{results/latency/cloudguard.csv}. Removing any one policy family reduced F1 from 1.0000 to 0.8889 and recall from 1.0000 to 0.8000.

\section{Discussion}
Within the frozen benchmark, the predicates reproduced the selected mutations and the metric pipeline was internally consistent. The perfect classification values are an implementation sanity result, not evidence of production accuracy; the coverage-led baseline table also does not establish superiority. The contribution is a traceability workflow that links a SID to evidence, severity, heuristic risk deduction, control reference, and guidance, not a measured usability result. The 30/30 result refers to the offline source-replacement experiment and its structural and target-rule-absence checks, not to 30 browser repair previews. Sustainability benefits, developer time, and energy use were not measured.

\subsection{Remediation Validation}
The 30-case remediation experiment was performed by an offline script that applies predefined source-pattern replacements to positive synthetic benchmark cases. Each replacement is checked for structural integrity and for the absence of the target policy pattern after modification. This experiment is separate from the browser repair interface. The browser interface provides deterministic repair previews for public S3 access indicators, RDS public-access flags, and disabled RDS encryption; unrestricted ingress and wildcard IAM cases remain subject to manual review.

The substitutions used in the offline experiment are controlled test transformations and are not necessarily appropriate for every deployment. In particular, replacing an unrestricted CIDR or wildcard IAM action with a fixed value does not prove that the result matches the application's intended network access or least-privilege requirements. The 30/30 result therefore measures success on the experiment's structural and target-rule checks, not semantic validity, deployment safety, or general automatic-repair quality.

\subsection{Leave-One-Policy-Out Ablation}
\begin{samepage}
The full system produced precision 1.0000, recall 1.0000, and F1 1.0000. Removing any one of the five policy rules produced precision 1.0000, recall 0.8000, and F1 0.8889, with six false negatives. Each of the five policy families contributes six positive cases in the synthetic benchmark. These results show sensitivity to removal of individual rules within the synthetic benchmark, not general model robustness.
\end{samepage}

\subsection{Limitations and Evidence Boundary}
The package contains no human-participant study, production configurations, native IaC validation logs, or large external benchmark. Repair is limited to controlled source-pattern previews, and pattern matching can miss variables, modules, intrinsic functions, indirection, or uncommon syntax. The score is a transparent prioritization aid, not a calibrated risk model.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
CloudGuard is a compact AWS IaC analyzer for local review of selected Terraform and CloudFormation conditions. It demonstrates stable policy identifiers, visible evidence, heuristic risk deductions, generated metrics, controlled remediation checks, and component sensitivity on a project-team synthetic benchmark. The results support regression testing and demonstration, not a production security guarantee. Future work should evaluate independently reviewed external configurations, validated semantic mappings across tools, native Terraform and CloudFormation validation, and measured developer or sustainability outcomes.
